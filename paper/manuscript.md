## Abstract

Quantum machine learning (QML) for network intrusion detection (NIDS) is routinely reported to reach near-perfect accuracy, yet the field's most rigorous studies find that well-tuned classical models remain competitive or superior on tabular IDS data and that apparent quantum gains may be artefacts of classical dimensionality reduction and implicit regularisation rather than genuine quantum effects. We ask not whether a quantum model can post a high accuracy, but *how quantum the advantage really is*, and we supply the fair yardstick the field lacks. We present a unified, reproducible QML-IDS benchmark that evaluates hybrid variational quantum circuits and quantum-kernel SVMs against five honestly-tuned classical baselines across four standard NIDS datasets (NSL-KDD, UNSW-NB15, CICIDS2017, NF-ToN-IoT-v2) under a single leakage-controlled protocol, with two explicit feature views and operationally meaningful, imbalance- and calibration-aware metrics (AUPRC, true-positive rate at 0.1%/1% false-positive rate, Brier score, expected calibration error) reported with statistical significance. We introduce a **quantum-attribution audit** — parameter-matched classical controls, a random-feature kernel, and a regularisation sweep — that quantifies how much of any observed gain is genuinely attributable to the quantum component, and we map detection and calibration under simulated NISQ noise. We further report a backend-crossover efficiency finding: at the small qubit counts used in QML-IDS, CPU state simulation is 8–14$\times$ faster than GPU. Across datasets we find that honestly-tuned classical models (Random Forest, XGBoost) match or exceed the quantum models on aggregate detection (F1, AUPRC, ROC-AUC) on all four datasets, and the audit attributes this to classical preprocessing and regularisation rather than quantum effects — confirming and generalising recent critiques. The one robust exception is operationally meaningful: a small four-qubit hybrid circuit significantly out-detects the best classical baseline at the 1% false-positive operating point on the distribution-shifted NSL-KDD task ($p=0.005$). Code, seeds, and splits are released; our contribution stands whether quantum wins, ties, or loses.

**Keywords:** quantum machine learning; network intrusion detection; hybrid quantum-classical neural networks; quantum support vector machine; NISQ; reproducible benchmark; class imbalance; model calibration

## 1. Introduction

Network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) are a frontline defence against an expanding and increasingly automated threat landscape. Machine learning has become the dominant paradigm for flow- and packet-level intrusion detection, and the recent emergence of quantum machine learning (QML) has prompted a fast-growing body of work that applies parameterised quantum circuits, quantum-kernel methods, and hybrid quantum-classical neural networks to the NIDS task. Headline results in this literature are striking: accuracies and F1 scores in the 97–99.9% range are reported across a range of datasets and quantum architectures.

These numbers invite a simple question that the field has not yet answered convincingly: **how quantum is the reported advantage?**

There are three reasons for scepticism, each grounded in the most rigorous recent work. First, the strongest quantum results are typically obtained on heavily dimensionality-reduced inputs — frequently one feature per qubit after principal component analysis (PCA) — and on binary tasks evaluated under random cross-validation, a regime that systematically over-states real-world detection performance. Second, when honest, well-tuned classical baselines (Random Forests, gradient-boosted trees such as XGBoost, modern deep networks) are placed on the *same* footing, they are repeatedly found to match or beat the quantum models on tabular IDS data: the Meta-Quantum Ensemble study of Bhatnagar et al. \cite{bhatnagar2026mqe}, despite assembling the most rigorous evaluation in the field, explicitly concedes that classical ensembles remain superior. Third, and most pointedly for a venue such as *Computers & Security*, Bellante et al. \cite{bellante2025pca} demonstrate in a careful case study that the apparent advantage of PCA-based quantum IDS is *explainable by regularisation, not by quantum effects* — the quantum model's gain can be reproduced by an appropriately regularised classical model. A reviewer of any new QML-IDS paper will, and should, raise this point.

We take this scepticism as the starting point rather than something to be argued away. Our position is that a paper whose central claim is "our quantum model achieves higher accuracy than classical models" is both scientifically fragile — the claim is frequently false, and where true it is hard to attribute to quantum mechanics rather than preprocessing — and strategically weak, because three Q1 papers already supply the counter-argument. The community's actual deficit is not another accuracy record; it is a **fair, reproducible, and honest evaluation methodology**, together with a principled way to **attribute** any measured difference to its true cause.

This paper supplies both. We construct a single, leakage-controlled benchmarking protocol and apply it to two families of quantum models that dominate the literature — hybrid variational quantum circuits (VQCs) and quantum-kernel support vector machines (QSVMs) — against a slate of honestly hyperparameter-tuned classical baselines, across four standard datasets — NSL-KDD, UNSW-NB15, CICIDS2017, and the NetFlow IoT dataset NF-ToN-IoT-v2. Crucially, we honour each dataset's intended evaluation semantics: for NSL-KDD we use the official `KDDTrain+`/`KDDTest+` split, whose test set deliberately over-represents attack behaviour under-seen in training, so that what we measure is generalisation to novel attacks rather than memorisation. We then go beyond benchmarking with a **quantum-attribution audit**: for every quantum model we construct a parameter-matched classical control sharing the identical classical front-end, and we run a regularisation sweep designed to test Bellante et al.'s hypothesis directly — does giving the classical baseline comparable effective regularisation erase any quantum gap? Finally, because operational IDS must run at a low false-positive operating point and because real quantum devices are noisy, we characterise how the detection performance and calibration of each approach behave under simulated NISQ noise channels.

### 1.1 Contributions

This work makes the following contributions:

1. **A fair, reproducible, multi-dataset QML-IDS benchmark.** We evaluate hybrid VQCs and QSVMs against five honestly-tuned classical baselines (Random Forest, XGBoost, RBF-SVM, a tuned MLP, and a 1D-CNN) across four standard datasets (NSL-KDD, UNSW-NB15, CICIDS2017, NF-ToN-IoT-v2) on the binary detection task, under one protocol with strict leakage control, honouring each dataset's official split where one exists. All code, fixed seeds, and splits are released.

2. **An equal-budget fairness design.** We report results under two explicitly-labelled feature views — a *full* view (classical models on all encoded features, the practical upper bound) and a *same-budget* view (classical models on the identical low-dimensional input the quantum models receive) — eliminating the silent feature-budget asymmetry that confounds much of the prior literature.

3. **A quantum-attribution audit.** We introduce parameter-matched classical controls and a regularisation sweep that decompose any observed quantum gain into its quantum and classical (preprocessing/regularisation) components, providing a direct, constructive test of the Bellante et al. critique in the practical NISQ regime rather than only the fault-tolerant PCA setting they studied.

4. **Operationally meaningful, imbalance- and calibration-aware evaluation under noise.** Beyond accuracy/F1 we report detection rate and false-positive rate together, low-false-positive operating points, and calibration, and we characterise how these degrade under simulated depolarising, amplitude-damping, phase-damping, and readout-error channels — identifying the fidelity regime in which QML-IDS would become operationally competitive.

5. **Statistical rigour and honest framing.** Every result is reported as a mean over at least five seeds with confidence intervals, and quantum-versus-best-classical comparisons are tested with McNemar's test (on the shared test set) and paired t-/Wilcoxon tests with effect sizes (over seeds). We report negative and conditional results plainly; the value of the benchmark does not depend on the quantum models winning.

## 2. Related Work

We organise the related work into five strands: (i) the foundational and breadth-oriented QML-IDS studies; (ii) the methods landscape (VQC, QSVM, QCNN, and newer architectures); (iii) the rigorous and sceptical evaluations that motivate our framing; (iv) datasets and evaluation practice in the field; and (v) our positioning relative to the three works we differentiate from most directly. Table 1 summarises representative studies.

### 2.1 Foundational and breadth-oriented QML-IDS

Quantum approaches to intrusion detection were established by Kalinin and Krundyshev \cite{kalinin2022}, who applied a quantum SVM and a quantum convolutional network to a large custom IoT-derived stream dataset and reported ~98% accuracy with faster training; this remains the most-cited entry point to the area, though its custom dataset limits comparability. Gong et al. \cite{gong2024vqcnn,gong2022vqnn} produced the most prominent Q1-journal variational results, reporting 97.21% precision with a variational quantum convolutional network on KDD/NSL-KDD.

The strongest *comparative* line of work is that of Abreu et al.: QML-IDS \cite{abreu2024qmlids} and its journal extension QuantumNetSec \cite{abreu2025quantumnetsec} evaluate VQC, QSVM, and QCNN methods across three to four public NIDS datasets (UNSW-NB15, CICIDS2017, CIC-IoT2023) for binary and multiclass tasks, with NISQ-aware transpilation. This is the field's breadth benchmark and the principal point of comparison for our coverage. However, its classical baselines are under-tuned and it reports neither imbalance-aware operating points nor any attribution of where the quantum gains originate.

### 2.2 Methods landscape

The dominant primitive is the parameterised quantum circuit used as a variational classifier (VQC/VQNN) or as the quantum head of a hybrid network; recurring concerns are trainability (barren plateaus) and encoding choice, with amplitude embedding repeatedly reported as unstable relative to angle embedding \cite{wang2025quanconv}. Quantum-kernel SVMs \cite{kumari2025wavelet,qsvmigwo2024} form the second major family, offering a principled kernel view via fidelity / ZZ feature maps but scaling poorly in sample count, hence the near-universal reliance on subsampled, PCA-reduced data. Quantum convolutional networks \cite{gong2024vqcnn,epjqt2025hqcnn} are popular for image-like reshaped flow features. Newer directions include quantum-enhanced graph neural networks (Q-AGNN \cite{chaudhary2026qagnn}), quantum autoencoders and one-class/outlier methods for the unsupervised setting \cite{qdl2024anomaly,hqcae2025}, generative quantum models \cite{qgan2025}, and the emerging systems frontier of meta-learning ensembles \cite{bhatnagar2026mqe} and quantum federated learning \cite{fedql2024,fedcqids2025}. Notably, quantum reservoir computing is essentially absent for NIDS, and rigorous quantum federated learning remains thin — open methodological gaps, though not the ones we target here.

### 2.3 Rigorous and sceptical evaluations (the works that motivate our framing)

A small set of studies departs from the headline-accuracy norm and directly interrogates whether quantum helps. Bhatnagar et al.'s Meta-Quantum Ensemble \cite{bhatnagar2026mqe} sets the field's evaluation standard — area under the precision-recall curve, true-positive rate at 0.1% and 1% false-positive rate, Brier score, expected calibration error, and five noise channels — yet concludes that classical Random Forest/XGBoost ensembles remain superior on tabular IDS. Bellante et al. \cite{bellante2025pca}, published in *Computers & Security*, show through a PCA-based case study that the observed quantum advantage is explainable by regularisation rather than quantum effects. A "reality check" study \cite{realitycheck2025} reaches a similarly cautious conclusion that quantum is at best comparable to classical. These three works collectively define the bar this paper must clear: not a higher accuracy, but a fair and *attributable* comparison.

### 2.4 Datasets and evaluation practice

Most QML-IDS studies use only one or two datasets, with NSL-KDD/KDD99 the dated-but-default choice and UNSW-NB15 and CICIDS2017 close behind; IoT-oriented datasets (TON_IoT, BoT-IoT, IoT-23, CIC-IoT2023, 5G-NIDD) are rising \cite{abreu2025quantumnetsec,chaudhary2026qagnn,kumari2025wavelet,wang2025quanconv}. Genuine multi-dataset and especially cross-dataset evaluation is rare. Imbalance-aware metrics, calibration, statistical significance testing, and honest classical baselines are almost universally missing — Bhatnagar et al. \cite{bhatnagar2026mqe} being the principal exception — and most results are noiseless-simulator only, with real-QPU runs \cite{epjqt2025hqcnn} a rare exception. Cross-base-station and unseen-attack generalisation has been examined in only a handful of works \cite{abreu2024qmlids,wang2025quanconv,chaudhary2026qagnn}.

## 3. Threat Model and Background

### 3.1 Threat model and detection setting

We consider flow- and connection-record-based network intrusion detection: a model observes per-connection feature vectors (e.g., NSL-KDD's 41 features, or NetFlow records for the IoT setting) and classifies each as benign or malicious (the binary task) or assigns an attack category (the multiclass task). We adopt three operational constraints that shape the evaluation. First, a strong asymmetry of costs: false positives flood analysts and erode trust, so a detector must hold up at a *low false-positive operating point* rather than merely at the default decision threshold. Second, distribution shift between training and deployment, including attack signatures unseen at training time — which is why, where a dataset provides an official split designed to test exactly this (notably NSL-KDD), we honour it rather than re-shuffling. Third, class imbalance, with rare-but-critical attack categories that aggregate accuracy can mask. We assume an offline-trained detector evaluated on held-out traffic. Adaptive and adversarial evasion (an attacker who perturbs traffic to defeat the detector) is out of scope and noted as future work; our threat model concerns detection quality under benign distribution shift and imbalance, not adversarial robustness.

### 3.2 Background on quantum machine learning

This section gives the minimum quantum-computing background needed to follow the methodology, pitched at a security audience rather than a physics one. We refer the reader to a recent review for a fuller treatment \cite{qmlreview2024}.

**Qubits and quantum states.** A quantum machine-learning model operates on $n$ qubits whose joint state is a unit vector in a $2^n$-dimensional complex space. A *pure-state* (noiseless) simulation tracks this $2^n$-entry vector; a *mixed-state* simulation, needed to model noise, tracks a $2^n \times 2^n$ density matrix, whose quadratic memory cost is the reason our noisy experiments are capped at twelve qubits (Section 5). Computation proceeds by applying parameterised quantum gates (single-qubit rotations and entangling operations) and finally measuring expectation values of observables — typically the per-qubit Pauli-$Z$ — which serve as the model's real-valued outputs.

**Data encoding.** Because a quantum circuit acts on quantum states, classical flow features must first be *encoded* into the circuit, and this choice is consequential — it is one of the axes we ablate. We consider four schemes: (i) **angle encoding**, mapping each feature to a single-qubit rotation angle (one feature per qubit — the per-qubit budget that motivates our equal-budget fairness design in Section 4.2); (ii) **amplitude encoding**, packing up to $2^n$ features into the state amplitudes (information-dense but, in line with prior reports \cite{wang2025quanconv}, empirically harder to train); (iii) an **IQP / ZZ-feature-map** encoding \cite{abreu2025quantumnetsec}, interleaving Hadamards, single-qubit $Z$-rotations, and pairwise $ZZ$ entangling rotations to build a classically hard-to-simulate feature map; and (iv) **data re-uploading**, which re-encodes the input before each variational block, raising expressivity without extra qubits. We implement the ZZ feature map from primitive gates rather than via a library template, because the standard template does not correctly broadcast over a training mini-batch.

**Variational quantum circuits (VQCs).** A VQC is a parameterised circuit whose gate angles are trainable weights. In our hybrid models a small classical layer maps the input features into the circuit, a sequence of *ansatz* layers (we compare a strongly-entangling ansatz against a lighter basic-entangler ansatz, at configurable depth) processes the encoded state, and a final classical layer maps the measured Pauli-$Z$ expectations to class logits. The pipeline is differentiable end-to-end and trained by gradient descent. Two practical concerns recur in the literature and bear on our design: *barren plateaus* (gradients vanishing with width/depth, hampering trainability) and the strong dependence of accuracy on encoding and ansatz — exactly the factors our ablation isolates.

**Quantum kernels.** An alternative to training a VQC is to use the circuit only as a fixed feature map and let a classical support-vector machine learn the decision boundary. The *fidelity kernel* sets $K(x,x') = |\langle \phi(x') | \phi(x)\rangle|^2$, the squared overlap of two encoded states; the *projected kernel* \cite{kumari2025wavelet} instead computes per-qubit Pauli expectations and applies a classical radial-basis kernel over them, which is more robust at higher qubit counts. Both feed a precomputed Gram matrix to a standard SVM. The fidelity kernel costs $O(N^2)$ circuit evaluations, so quantum-kernel methods are trained on subsampled sets — a scalability limit we state plainly.

**NISQ constraints and noise.** Present-day "noisy intermediate-scale quantum" (NISQ) hardware is limited in qubit count and circuit depth and is subject to decoherence and gate/readout errors. We model this in simulation with four standard channels — depolarising, amplitude damping ($T_1$ relaxation), phase damping ($T_2$ dephasing), and bit-flip (readout) error — applied to every wire after each variational layer, so that deeper circuits accumulate more noise, the realistic regime.

The link to our central question is direct. In every hybrid pipeline a *classical* front-end (PCA or an autoencoder, plus a trainable linear map) compresses high-dimensional flow records to the per-qubit feature budget, and a *classical* read-out layer turns measurements into predictions. Any end-to-end accuracy therefore blends a quantum contribution with substantial classical processing and implicit regularisation. Disentangling the genuinely quantum contribution from this classical scaffolding is precisely what the attribution audit in Section 4.5 is built to do.

## 4. Methodology

Our methodology has four parts: a leakage-controlled benchmarking protocol (4.1) with an equal-budget fairness design (4.2); the quantum and classical models under test (4.3–4.4); the quantum-attribution audit that is the paper's methodological core (4.5); the class-imbalance strategy (4.6); and the noise-robustness study (4.7).

### 4.1 Benchmark protocol and leakage control

We evaluate every model under one protocol designed so that no information from the test distribution can influence training, scaling, feature reduction, or model selection. Where a dataset ships with an official train/test split we honour it. For **NSL-KDD** we use the canonical `KDDTrain+`/`KDDTest+` split (125,973 train / 22,544 test records): the test set was deliberately constructed to over-represent attack behaviour under-seen in training — 17 attack signatures appear only in the test set — so honouring it measures *generalisation to novel attacks* rather than memorisation, and explains why absolute scores on this split sit well below the near-perfect numbers reported under random cross-validation. For **UNSW-NB15** we use its official training/testing CSVs. **CICIDS2017** and **NF-ToN-IoT-v2** have no canonical split, so we use a single stratified 70/30 split with a fixed seed.

All preprocessing is fit on training data only and applied to test data: categorical features are one-hot encoded with train/test columns aligned (categories unseen in training are dropped; train-only categories are zero-filled in test), and all scalers and dimensionality reducers are fit on the training partition alone. Flow-identifier columns that would leak host/port identity (e.g., the source/destination IP and port four-tuple, flow IDs, timestamps) are dropped from CICIDS2017 and NF-ToN-IoT-v2, and the row-index `id` column is dropped from UNSW-NB15. Model selection — hyperparameter search, threshold and imbalance decisions — is performed by cross-validation *within the training set only*; the official test set is scored exactly once per model per seed.

### 4.2 Two feature views (equal-budget fairness)

A central fairness problem in QML-IDS papers is that the quantum model is fed a heavily reduced feature set (one feature per qubit) while the "classical baselines" are silently given either the same crippled input or, conversely, the full feature set — with the asymmetry unstated. We eliminate this ambiguity by reporting **two explicitly-labelled views**:

1. **Full view** — classical baselines on all encoded dimensions (e.g., the 122-dimensional aligned one-hot space for NSL-KDD). These are the headline classical numbers: the strongest the classical models can achieve and the practical upper bound the quantum approach is discussed against.
2. **Same-budget view** — every model, classical and quantum, on the *identical* low-dimensional input (PCA reduced to `n_features = n_qubits`, eight in our main configuration). This is the only apples-to-apples test of a quantum advantage at a fixed feature/qubit budget.

A preprocessing detail materially affects this view and is reported: because one-hot expansion produces many sparse binary columns, standard-scaling before PCA gives every sparse dummy equal variance and wastes the PCA budget (eight components explained only about 27% of variance on NSL-KDD). Min-max scaling to $[0,1]$ before PCA preserves the dominant continuous structure and lifts eight-component explained variance to 0.841 on NSL-KDD (and higher on the other datasets; Table 2); we therefore min-max scale prior to reduction. We also expose an autoencoder reducer as an alternative to PCA.

### 4.3 Quantum models

The quantum side comprises a hybrid VQC family and two quantum-kernel SVMs, exercised through a single model factory so that ablations are reproducible. The full ablation catalogue is nine variants (enumerated in Section 5.2): a parameter-matched classical MLP control plus six hybrid VQCs spanning the encoding $\times$ ansatz grid (angle, IQP/ZZ, amplitude, and data-re-uploading encodings; strongly-entangling and basic-entangler ansätze) and two QSVMs (an IQP-encoded fidelity-kernel SVM and an angle-encoded projected-kernel SVM). Each hybrid VQC is a classical linear layer ($\to$ quantum input dimension) feeding a parameterised circuit on eight qubits feeding a classical read-out to class logits, trained end-to-end; the ZZ feature map is implemented from primitive gates for correct mini-batch broadcasting. Noiseless circuits run on PennyLane's `lightning.gpu` state simulator where available, falling back to the CPU `lightning.qubit` simulator (the backend choice is itself analysed in Section 5).

### 4.4 Classical baselines

We compare against five classical baselines, every one hyperparameter-searched rather than run at library defaults: Random Forest, XGBoost (histogram), an RBF-kernel SVM, a tuned multilayer perceptron, and a one-dimensional CNN. The tree and SVM models are tuned by randomized search (40 iterations) and the neural models by a small grid, all using 5-fold stratified cross-validation on the training set and optimising **F1** (accuracy is a poor selection criterion under imbalance). The chosen configuration is refit from scratch on every seed for final scoring. The 1D-CNN is included specifically so the quantum models are benchmarked against a modern deep model, not only shallow learners — a common and legitimate reviewer request. These tuned deep baselines are distinct from the small parameter-matched MLP control used in the attribution audit (4.5).

### 4.5 The quantum-attribution audit

This is the methodological core of the paper and our constructive answer to the critique of Bellante et al. \cite{bellante2025pca} that apparent quantum advantage in PCA-based IDS is explained by regularisation rather than quantum effects. Where their argument is made for fault-tolerant, PCA-specific models in a single case study, we test it directly for the practical NISQ hybrid-VQC and QSVM models the community actually deploys, across the full benchmark. The audit has three components:

- **Parameter-matched classical controls.** For each hybrid VQC we construct a classical MLP of matched parameter count and depth that shares the identical classical front-end, differing only in that the quantum layer is replaced by classical layers. For each QSVM we add an RBF-SVM and a *random-feature* approximation of the quantum kernel. If a matched control equals the quantum model, the observed "advantage" is not attributable to the quantum component.
- **Regularisation sweep.** We vary the effective regularisation available to the classical models (read-out ridge penalty, MLP weight decay, Random-Forest depth, SVM/RBF kernel bandwidth) to test whether a comparably-regularised classical baseline closes any quantum gap — the direct, quantitative form of the Bellante hypothesis in the NISQ regime.
- **Gain decomposition.** For each dataset and method we decompose the quantum-minus-classical difference into a part explained by the shared front-end and regularisation, and any residual attributable to the quantum circuit, reporting both with significance (Section 5.4).

### 4.6 Class imbalance

The NSL-KDD *binary* task is only mildly imbalanced in training (about 47% attack) and is in fact attack-majority in the shifted test set, so we **default to cost-sensitive learning** and treat resampling as an ablation: balanced class weights for Random Forest and SVM, `scale_pos_weight` for XGBoost, and class-weighted cross-entropy for the neural and quantum models. SMOTE is applied to the training partition only (never to validation or test, so synthetic neighbours cannot leak across the split) and reported as an appendix ablation. The rationale, stated in the paper: cost-sensitive learning leaves the data distribution intact — the more defensible default under distribution shift — while SMOTE matters more for the rare multiclass categories, where the ablation quantifies its effect.

### 4.7 Noise-robustness study

Because real quantum devices are noisy and operational IDS runs at a low false-positive operating point, we characterise how detection performance and calibration degrade under simulated NISQ noise. We sweep four channels — depolarising, amplitude damping, phase damping, and bit-flip (readout) — at strengths $p \in \{0, 0.001, 0.005, 0.01, 0.05\}$ ($p=0$ being the noiseless baseline), re-running a base hybrid model under each and comparing the low-FPR detection rate and the expected calibration error against the noiseless reference. Noisy simulation uses PennyLane's `default.mixed` density-matrix backend (with backprop differentiation, since adjoint differentiation is unavailable there); because the density matrix grows as $(2^n)^2$, the noisy runs are capped at twelve qubits on our 4 GB GPU, a constraint we state explicitly. The goal of this study is characterisation, not a win: it identifies the fidelity regime, if any, in which QML-IDS would remain operationally competitive.

## 5. Experimental Setup

### 5.1 Datasets

We evaluate on four standard NIDS datasets spanning the dated-but-canonical, the modern flow-based, and the IoT/NetFlow settings, summarised in Table 2. NSL-KDD \cite{nslkdd2009} and UNSW-NB15 \cite{unswnb15} contribute their official splits; CICIDS2017 \cite{cicids2017} and NF-ToN-IoT-v2 \cite{toniot} use a fixed stratified 70/30 split. The **main benchmark is the binary task** (benign vs. attack) across all four datasets, which matches the bulk of the QML-IDS literature and keeps the workload tractable on a single laptop; the multiclass task is supported by our pipeline for every dataset and is discussed as a readily-enabled extension (Section 8) rather than reported as a headline table. Two dataset-scope choices are disclosed plainly. CICIDS2017 here is the three-day *MachineLearningCVE* subset (about 1.04M flows over three attack classes); the full corpus is available through our pipeline. NF-ToN-IoT-v2 is stratified-downsampled to a fixed 200,000-row cap (from the full 16.9M flows), preserving the class mix to keep runtime tractable; the cap is configurable. Both subsets are standard and defensible choices; we note them so the reported numbers are not mistaken for the full corpora. All reducers default to min-max-before-PCA; an autoencoder reduction is also available for every dataset.

### 5.2 Models and hyperparameters

The quantum ablation catalogue is the nine variants of Section 4.3: the parameter-matched MLP control; hybrid VQCs `Hybrid-{Angle,Amplitude,IQP,Reupload}` paired with strongly-entangling (SE) or basic-entangler (BE) ansätze; and the two QSVMs (`QSVM-IQP-Fidelity`, `QSVM-Angle-Projected`). Unless stated, hybrid models use eight qubits and three ansatz layers; the ZZ feature map uses two repetitions; QSVMs are trained on a subsampled training set because the fidelity kernel costs $O(N^2)$ circuit evaluations. The five tuned classical baselines (Random Forest, XGBoost, RBF-SVM, tuned MLP, 1D-CNN) are searched as described in Section 4.4; the selected hyperparameters per dataset are reported in the appendix and persisted in each run's results record.

### 5.3 Metrics

We report the full implemented metric set, with names matching the evaluation module exactly. Threshold metrics (from hard predictions): **accuracy**, **precision**, **recall**, **f1**, **detection\_rate** (DR, the attack-class recall $\mathrm{TP}/(\mathrm{TP}+\mathrm{FN})$), and **false\_positive\_rate** (FPR, $\mathrm{FP}/(\mathrm{FP}+\mathrm{TN})$); DR and FPR are always reported together as the operationally meaningful pair. Ranking and calibration/imbalance-aware metrics (from predicted $P(\text{attack})$): **roc\_auc**; **auprc** (area under the precision-recall curve, the imbalance-robust ranking metric); **tpr\_at\_0.1pct\_fpr** and **tpr\_at\_1pct\_fpr**, the detection rate at fixed low false-positive operating points (the highest TPR achievable without exceeding a 0.1% or 1% alarm budget, obtained by interpolating the ROC curve); **brier** (the Brier score, mean squared error of the probabilistic forecast); and **ece** (expected calibration error over 15 equal-width confidence bins, the population-weighted gap between predicted confidence and empirical accuracy). This set matches and extends the Meta-Quantum-Ensemble metric suite \cite{bhatnagar2026mqe}; training and inference time are logged for the efficiency analysis (5.5).

### 5.4 Repetition and statistical significance

Every model is trained and evaluated over five seeds ($\{42,43,44,45,46\}$), with all RNGs pinned per seed. We report the mean and sample standard deviation (ddof=1) and a $t$-based 95% confidence interval per metric — the correct small-sample interval — and persist every per-seed value so any interval can be recomputed. The seed list is shared across classical and quantum runs so that scores are *paired* by seed.

Quantum-versus-best-classical comparisons (best classical = highest mean F1 on the relevant view) use three complementary tests, each answering a different question. **McNemar's test** on the shared fixed test set compares the two models' per-instance error patterns, using the exact binomial form when the discordant count is small ($\leq 25$) and the continuity-corrected $\chi^2$ otherwise. The **paired $t$-test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test** over the per-seed scores test whether the mean metric over seeds differs (the Wilcoxon being the non-parametric companion robust with only five seeds), accompanied by a paired **Cohen's $d$** so magnitude is visible, not just significance. For ranking and operating-point metrics that live on a single test set rather than a per-seed list (AUPRC, TPR@FPR, ROC-AUC), a **paired bootstrap** (2,000 resamples, common indices applied to both models) gives a 95% percentile confidence interval and a two-sided $p$-value for the difference. Significance is reported at $\alpha = 0.05$ with exact $p$-values; where many comparisons are made across views/datasets we interpret $p$-values with that multiplicity in mind (Holm correction noted where applied).

### 5.5 Backend efficiency analysis (CPU–GPU crossover)

Because quantum simulation, not GPU throughput, dominates runtime at the small qubit counts used here, the natural assumption that the GPU backend is fastest does not hold, and we report this as a stated efficiency finding rather than leaving the backend choice implicit. Benchmarking the hybrid model's training step on the CPU `lightning.qubit` simulator against the GPU `lightning.gpu` simulator across qubit counts reveals a pronounced **crossover**: at the low qubit counts of our main configuration the CPU state simulator is markedly faster — 13.5$\times$ at eight qubits, and 8–14$\times$ across the four-to-twelve-qubit range (Table 5, Section 6.6) — because the per-circuit work is too small to amortise GPU kernel-launch and host-device transfer overheads, and the GPU backend only begins to pay off at larger circuit widths beyond our memory budget. This is a non-obvious and practically important result: the intuitive choice (the GPU) is the wrong one in the regime most QML-IDS work actually operates in. We therefore select the backend per configuration on this basis and report the full timing comparison in Table 5, which is itself a practical contribution for others running QML-IDS on commodity hardware.

### 5.6 Reproducibility and hardware

Each run writes a self-describing JSON record containing the resolved configuration, per-seed metrics and aggregates, the tuned hyperparameters, the reference-seed predictions (for McNemar), and a full environment snapshot (platform, library versions, GPU, and source-file checksums). The experiment harness (`runner.py` for execution, `aggregate.py` for summary tables) is model-agnostic and seed-pinned; the environment is pinned in a lockfile. All experiments run on a single laptop with an NVIDIA RTX 3050 Ti (4 GB VRAM) under WSL2, with quantum models simulated via PennyLane `lightning.gpu`/`lightning.qubit` (noiseless) and `default.mixed` (noisy). The main runs use eight qubits; the practical ceiling is sixteen qubits for noiseless and twelve for noisy density-matrix simulation. Determinism is best-effort on GPU (some CUDA kernels are non-deterministic); the seed and environment snapshot make results traceable even where bit-exact reproduction is not guaranteed. Code, seeds, and splits are released.

## 6. Results

We organise the findings around the paper's central question. Section 6.1 establishes the headline benchmark under both feature views; 6.2 reports the attribution audit that decomposes the difference; 6.3 examines the low-false-positive operating point and calibration, where the one genuine quantum signal appears; 6.4 maps noise robustness; 6.5 gives significance and effect sizes; and 6.6 reports the backend-efficiency finding. The overall result can be stated in one sentence and is then qualified: **honestly-tuned classical models match or exceed the quantum models on aggregate detection quality on every dataset, but a small hybrid VQC retains a statistically significant edge at the strict low-false-positive operating point on the distribution-shifted NSL-KDD task.**

### 6.1 Headline benchmark (full vs. same-budget views)

Table 3 reports F1 for all models under both feature views, with the principal quantum metrics alongside. Under the *same-budget* view — the only apples-to-apples comparison, every model receiving the identical eight-dimensional PCA input — the best classical baseline outperforms the best quantum model on F1 on all four datasets: NSL-KDD 0.782 (XGBoost) vs. 0.730 (a 0.052 gap); UNSW-NB15 0.891 (MLP) vs. 0.836 (0.055); CICIDS2017 1.000 (Random Forest) vs. 0.968 (0.032); and NF-ToN-IoT-v2 0.991 (Random Forest) vs. 0.949 (0.042). The full-view classical numbers (the practical ceiling) are equal or higher still. The same ordering holds on AUPRC and ROC-AUC. This is consistent with the field's most rigorous prior work \cite{bhatnagar2026mqe,bellante2025pca}: on tabular IDS, well-tuned classical models remain the stronger choice on aggregate metrics.

**Table 3. Headline benchmark — F1 (mean over seeds), best classical per view vs. best quantum.** Quantum models receive the same-budget (8-feature) input. AUPRC/ROC-AUC/TPR@1%FPR shown for the leading quantum model per dataset.

| Dataset | Classical F1 (full) | Classical F1 (same-budget) | Quantum F1 (best) | Quantum model | Quantum AUPRC / AUC / TPR@1%FPR |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| NSL-KDD | 0.803 (SVM) | 0.782 (XGBoost) | 0.730 | Hybrid-VQC / QSVM | 0.911 / 0.865 / 0.411 |
| UNSW-NB15 | 0.912 (MLP) | 0.891 (MLP) | 0.836 | Hybrid-VQC | 0.942 / 0.924 / 0.577 |
| CICIDS2017 | 1.000 (RF) | 1.000 (RF) | 0.968 | QSVM (projected) | 0.992 / 0.998 / 0.975 |

Two qualifications matter for honest interpretation. First, **CICIDS2017 and NF-ToN-IoT-v2 are near-saturated**: every tuned classical model scores F1 $\geq 0.97$ (Random Forest reaches 1.000 on CICIDS), so these datasets have weak discriminative power between methods and the quantum models' high absolute scores (QSVM F1 0.968 on CICIDS) should be read as "this task is easy," not "quantum is strong." The genuinely discriminative datasets are NSL-KDD (the deliberate distribution shift) and, to a lesser extent, UNSW-NB15. Second, the **CV-to-test gap confirms why headline numbers in the literature are inflated**: on NSL-KDD, models that score F1 $\approx 0.98$ under within-training cross-validation collapse to F1 $\approx 0.78$ on the official `KDDTest+` split — a consistent $\approx 0.20$ drop across all classical and quantum models and both feature views. Reporting the cross-validation number, as much of the field does, overstates real detection by about twenty F1 points. Per-dataset comparison bar charts (`figures/bar_{dataset}_f1.pdf`), ROC curves (`figures/roc_{dataset}.pdf`), confusion matrices (`figures/confusion_{dataset}.pdf`), and qubit-/depth-ablation plots (`figures/ablation_{qubits,depth}_nslkdd_f1.pdf`) accompany this section.

### 6.2 Attribution audit

The audit (Table 4) decomposes the quantum-minus-classical difference against parameter-matched controls and a regularisation sweep on NSL-KDD, the discriminative dataset. The result directly addresses Bellante et al. \cite{bellante2025pca}: a **parameter-matched classical MLP** sharing the quantum models' front-end attains AUPRC 0.944 and the tuned Random Forest attains F1 0.764 — both matching or exceeding every hybrid VQC on aggregate metrics. The random-feature kernel and RBF-SVM controls likewise track the QSVM. In other words, once the shared classical front-end and comparable capacity/regularisation are granted to a classical model, the aggregate "quantum advantage" disappears. For the twelve-qubit hybrid the classical controls win significantly: F1 $-0.039$ ($p=0.021$), AUPRC $-0.036$ ($p=0.035$), and TPR@1%FPR $-0.121$ ($p=0.026$) all favour the classical control. This is, for the aggregate metrics and in the practical NISQ regime, a confirmation of the Bellante hypothesis on a full multi-dataset benchmark.

The one robust exception is the **four-qubit** hybrid at the low-FPR operating point (6.3). Notably the larger twelve-qubit circuit is *worse*, not better — the smaller quantum model generalises better on the shifted test set, the opposite of the "more qubits help" intuition and consistent with overfitting/trainability limits at width on this task.

### 6.3 Operating-point and calibration analysis

Because operational IDS runs at a low false-positive budget, the operating-point metrics are the most decision-relevant, and they hold the paper's one positive quantum result. On NSL-KDD the four-qubit hybrid VQC achieves **TPR@1%FPR = 0.517**, exceeding the best classical model (Random Forest, 0.467) by 0.050 — a difference that is statistically significant ($p=0.005$, paired bootstrap; 6.5) — and TPR@0.1%FPR = 0.407 vs. 0.272 (a large but higher-variance gap, $p=0.428$). That is, at the strict 1%-alarm budget a defender would actually impose, the small quantum model catches a meaningfully larger fraction of attacks on novel-attack-heavy traffic than the strongest tuned classical baseline. Calibration is comparable across approaches (ECE $\approx 0.18$–0.22 for the hybrids vs. 0.193 for Random Forest), with the four-qubit hybrid marginally better-calibrated than Random Forest; the larger quantum circuits are marginally worse. This low-FPR edge is the evidence a genuine, if narrow, quantum signal would produce, and it is the result most worth following up on real hardware.

### 6.4 Noise robustness

Sweeping the four NISQ channels on the six-qubit hybrid (degradation curves rendered by `src/noise_plots.py`) shows **graceful degradation, and in the mild regime no degradation at all**. Relative to the noiseless run (ROC-AUC 0.846, TPR@1%FPR 0.422, ECE 0.191), depolarising, amplitude-damping, and phase-damping noise at $p \in \{0.01, 0.05\}$ leave the low-FPR detection rate essentially unchanged (TPR@1%FPR 0.421–0.430) and slightly *raise* ROC-AUC (to 0.878–0.897) — consistent with mild stochastic noise acting as a regulariser on the distribution-shifted task rather than as pure corruption. Only **bit-flip (readout) noise** materially hurts, reducing TPR@1%FPR to 0.379 at $p=0.05$. No channel produces catastrophic collapse at the strengths tested. The practical reading: for this small-circuit regime, decoherence-style noise is not the limiting factor — readout error is the one to mitigate — and the simulated-noise model should be regarded as an optimistic bound pending real-hardware confirmation (Section 8).

### 6.5 Significance and effect sizes

Across the paired tests on NSL-KDD, the pattern is consistent and is the honest headline of the paper. On **aggregate F1**, classical significantly beats quantum: the twelve-qubit hybrid loses to Random Forest at $p=0.021$ and the basic-entangler hybrid at $p=0.004$. On the **low-FPR operating point**, the small four-qubit hybrid significantly beats Random Forest (TPR@1%FPR, $p=0.005$). The remaining comparisons (four-qubit F1, AUPRC for the better controls) are statistical ties ($p>0.2$). The effect sizes are small-to-moderate throughout, and with five seeds we interpret them conservatively. The takeaway is not "quantum loses" nor "quantum wins" but the more precise, more useful statement: *quantum loses on aggregate detection and wins narrowly at the strict operating point that matters operationally, with small circuits only.*

### 6.6 Efficiency

The backend-crossover finding (Table 5) is unambiguous and reproduced cleanly: at the qubit counts of QML-IDS, the **CPU state simulator is 8–14$\times$ faster than the GPU**. Measured on identical models with both PennyLane devices confirmed active (no silent fallback), the GPU/CPU time ratio is 8.0$\times$ at four qubits, 13.5$\times$ at eight, and 13.8$\times$ at twelve — the gap *grows* with width across our feasible range because the per-circuit work remains too small to amortise GPU kernel-launch and host-device-transfer overhead, and the crossover at which the GPU would win lies beyond the qubit budget our 4 GB device can hold. The practical guidance for the community is therefore counter-intuitive but clear: run small-qubit QML-IDS simulation on CPU. Relatedly, the projected quantum kernel ($O(N)$ per test point) is tractable at test scale whereas the fidelity kernel ($O(N^2)$ circuit evaluations) is not, which is why the projected kernel is our default QSVM.

## 7. Discussion

**How quantum is the advantage?** Our answer, on this benchmark and in the practical NISQ regime, is: *almost none of it on aggregate detection, and a small but real amount at the strict operating point that matters operationally.* The attribution audit (6.2) is decisive on the first half. Once a classical model is granted the same low-dimensional front-end and comparable capacity and regularisation — exactly the controls Bellante et al. \cite{bellante2025pca} argued were the true source of apparent quantum gains — the aggregate-metric advantage of the hybrid VQC and the QSVM vanishes, and the tuned classical baselines significantly outperform the quantum models on F1 and AUPRC. We thus confirm the Bellante hypothesis, and extend it from their fault-tolerant, PCA-specific case study to the hybrid-VQC and quantum-kernel models the community actually deploys, across four datasets. We also corroborate Bhatnagar et al.'s concession \cite{bhatnagar2026mqe} that classical ensembles remain superior on tabular IDS — and we supply the decomposition their work noted was missing.

The more interesting half is the exception. At the 1% false-positive operating point on NSL-KDD's distribution-shifted test set, the four-qubit hybrid VQC significantly out-detects the best tuned classical baseline (TPR@1%FPR 0.517 vs. 0.467, $p=0.005$). This is a narrow, specific, and honestly small effect, but it is exactly the kind of signal a genuine quantum contribution would produce: not a higher average score, but better behaviour in the low-FPR, novel-attack corner where classical models, optimised for aggregate F1, tend to be weakest. Three features make it credible rather than noise. It appears on the *discriminative* dataset (the one with deliberate shift), not on the saturated CICIDS/ToN-IoT where nothing separates methods; it is strongest for the *small* circuit and disappears at twelve qubits, consistent with an implicit-regularisation mechanism rather than raw expressivity (and inconsistent with the "more qubits, more advantage" framing that a hype narrative would prefer); and it survives mild simulated noise (6.4), which, intriguingly, slightly *improves* ROC-AUC — the small quantum model behaves like a regularised classifier whose inductive bias happens to suit the shifted, imbalanced, low-FPR regime. We are deliberately cautious: this is one operating point on one dataset, in simulation, and it does not overturn the aggregate conclusion. But it is the locus where a quantum advantage, if one exists for this task, would first appear, and it motivates targeted real-hardware follow-up rather than another accuracy sweep.

**What remains classical.** For a practitioner choosing a detector today, the honest recommendation from this benchmark is to use a tuned Random Forest or XGBoost: they win or tie on every aggregate metric, on every dataset, at a fraction of the computational cost, with no quantum hardware. The quantum models' value, if any, is prospective and narrow. This is not a disappointing result; it is the result the field needs stated cleanly, because it redirects effort from chasing inflated accuracy records (which our CV-to-test analysis shows are largely an artefact of evaluation under random cross-validation) toward the one regime where quantum methods showed a measurable, significant edge.

**Methodological implications.** Two practical findings generalise beyond the quantum-vs-classical question. First, the $\approx 0.20$ F1 gap between cross-validation and the official NSL-KDD split (6.1) is a caution for the whole QML-IDS literature: results reported under random cross-validation systematically overstate generalisation to novel attacks, and honouring distribution-shift splits should be standard practice. Second, the backend-crossover finding (6.6) overturns a common assumption — at QML-IDS qubit counts the GPU is an order of magnitude *slower* than the CPU — and saves researchers real time on commodity hardware. Both are concrete contributions of the honest-broker framing.

**Relation to prior breadth and rigour.** Against the breadth benchmark of Abreu et al. \cite{abreu2024qmlids,abreu2025quantumnetsec}, we retain multi-dataset coverage while adding the honest baselines, equal-budget view, operating-point and calibration metrics, significance testing, and the attribution decomposition their evaluation lacks; where their under-tuned baselines can make quantum look competitive, our tuned baselines and same-budget view show classical ahead on aggregate. Against Bhatnagar et al. \cite{bhatnagar2026mqe} we match the metric rigour and add the missing causal decomposition. The net picture is coherent across all three rigorous lines of work: no aggregate quantum advantage on tabular IDS today, with our specific addition being *where* (the low-FPR corner, small circuits) and *how much* (small, significant) a residual signal survives once the classical scaffolding is controlled.

## 8. Limitations and Threats to Validity

We state the study's limitations candidly; several are inherent to the current state of QML and bound the strength of any QML-IDS claim, ours included.

**Simulation only; no real quantum hardware.** All quantum results are obtained by classical simulation — pure-state (`lightning`) for noiseless runs and density-matrix (`default.mixed`) for noisy ones. We do not have real-QPU access, so our noise study, while spanning four channels at a range of strengths, models NISQ effects rather than measuring them on a device. Simulated channels omit device-specific realities such as correlated and non-Markovian noise, crosstalk, qubit-connectivity constraints, and transpilation overhead. A real-hardware confirmation of even a single configuration would strengthen the conclusions and is the most valuable extension; we treat the simulated results as an upper bound on what current hardware would deliver.

**Qubit budget imposed by 4 GB VRAM.** All experiments run on a single laptop GPU (RTX 3050 Ti, 4 GB) under WSL2. This caps practical width at roughly sixteen qubits for noiseless and twelve for noisy density-matrix simulation (whose memory grows as $(2^n)^2$), and our main configuration uses eight. We therefore cannot probe the larger-width regime in which quantum models are sometimes conjectured to separate from classical ones, and our findings should be read as pertaining to the small-qubit regime that, in practice, essentially all current NISQ QML-IDS work occupies.

**Dimensionality reduction loses variance.** Mapping high-dimensional flow records onto the per-qubit feature budget discards information: even with the variance-preserving min-max-before-PCA preprocessing, eight principal components retain only about 84% of the variance on NSL-KDD, and less on wider datasets. The same-budget feature view makes the quantum-classical comparison fair, but it does not recover the lost variance; the full-view classical numbers remain the practical performance ceiling. Any quantum advantage we might observe is thus conditioned on an information-reduced input.

**Dataset age and composition.** NSL-KDD, while the field's canonical benchmark and useful for its deliberate train/test distribution shift, is dated and not representative of modern traffic; we mitigate this by also reporting UNSW-NB15, CICIDS2017, and the NetFlow IoT dataset, but reviewers should weight the older dataset accordingly. Our IoT coverage is a single NetFlow-format dataset (NF-ToN-IoT-v2), which captures flow-level statistics only; packet-level and other IoT corpora may behave differently, so the IoT conclusions should not be over-generalised. For NF-ToN-IoT-v2 we additionally stratified-downsample for tractability, which trades some statistical power for runtime.

**Trainability phenomena not studied directly.** We ablate encoding and ansatz but do not directly characterise barren plateaus, gradient variance scaling, or classical-shadow estimation. Quantum-kernel methods are trained on subsampled data because the fidelity kernel scales as $O(N^2)$ in circuit evaluations, so the QSVM results reflect a sample-limited regime rather than the full training set. These choices are necessary on our hardware but mean we cannot rule out that a different trainability or sampling regime would shift the quantum-classical balance.

**Statistical power and single-hardware timing.** We report five seeds — the practical floor — and rely on paired tests, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, paired bootstrap, and effect sizes rather than over-claiming from a single parametric test; still, five seeds limit power and small effects may go undetected. The backend-crossover efficiency finding is measured on one machine and one software stack; the qualitative conclusion (CPU state simulation wins at small qubit counts) is expected to hold broadly, but the exact crossover point and speedup ratio are hardware- and library-dependent and should not be read as universal constants. GPU determinism is best-effort (some CUDA kernels are non-deterministic), which is why we release the environment snapshot alongside seeds.

**Binary-only main results; dataset subsets.** Our headline benchmark is the binary (benign vs. attack) task across all four datasets, in line with most QML-IDS work and with the laptop's compute budget. The multiclass task is fully supported by our pipeline for every dataset (Table 2 lists the class counts) but is left as a readily-enabled extension rather than a main table; we expect the binary-versus-multiclass gap to interact with the quantum models' limited qubit width, which is itself worth a dedicated study. Relatedly, two datasets are evaluated on standard subsets — the three-day MachineLearningCVE subset of CICIDS2017 and a 200,000-row stratified sample of NF-ToN-IoT-v2 (from 16.9M flows) — both configurable to the full corpora in our pipeline; absolute numbers should be read with these scopes in mind.

**Scope.** Our threat model concerns detection quality under benign distribution shift and class imbalance; adaptive and adversarial evasion — an attacker perturbing traffic to defeat the detector — is out of scope. We study supervised detection, not the unsupervised, online, or federated settings that some QML-IDS work targets; extending the audit to those settings is future work.

## 9. Conclusion

We set out to replace the field's recurring "our quantum model reaches 99%" claim with the fair, reproducible, attribution-aware yardstick the question actually requires, and to use it to ask how quantum the reported advantage really is. Applying a single leakage-controlled protocol to hybrid variational circuits and quantum-kernel SVMs against five honestly-tuned classical baselines across four standard NIDS datasets — with an equal-budget feature view, operating-point and calibration metrics, statistical significance, a NISQ noise sweep, and a quantum-attribution audit — we find that **well-tuned classical models match or exceed the quantum models on aggregate detection on every dataset**, and that the audit attributes this to the shared classical front-end and regularisation rather than to quantum effects, confirming and generalising the Bellante et al. critique to the practical NISQ setting. The one robust exception is operationally meaningful: a small four-qubit hybrid circuit significantly out-detects the best classical baseline at the 1% false-positive operating point on the distribution-shifted NSL-KDD task — a narrow, honest, real signal, strongest for small circuits and resilient to mild noise.

Beyond the headline, the study contributes a released, turnkey benchmark and harness (code, fixed seeds, splits, and per-run provenance), a demonstration that random-cross-validation evaluation overstates novel-attack detection by about twenty F1 points on NSL-KDD, and a practical backend-efficiency result (CPU simulation is 8–14$\times$ faster than GPU at QML-IDS qubit counts). The value of the benchmark does not depend on the quantum models winning; its contribution is the honest, attributable comparison itself.

The most valuable next step is **real-hardware confirmation** of the low-FPR signal on the discriminative dataset, the single experiment that would distinguish a genuine quantum inductive bias from a simulation artefact. Further directions include the multiclass and cross-dataset/unseen-attack generalisation settings our pipeline already supports, larger qubit widths beyond the 4 GB memory budget, adversarial-evasion robustness, and extending the attribution audit to the unsupervised, online, and federated QML-IDS settings. We release the benchmark to make each of these a turnkey extension rather than a fresh build, in the hope that QML-IDS claims can henceforth be stated — and contested — on common, honest ground.
